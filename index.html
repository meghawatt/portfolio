GTM & lead generation

An AI-startup lead generation system

Web scraping is a supplemental need for most companies, which makes buyers hard to find. I designed a system to find and score them, then wrote the outreach. Of 100 founders we contacted, 10 took a call and 2 became customers.

Company
ScrapeHero
My role
Content & Marketing Strategist. I conceived and led it.
Timeline
2026
Worked with
Engineering

Results

  • ~2,300

    AI startups scored for fit, from 0 to 100

  • 100

    founders contacted, every one from a startup that scored 90

  • 10

    agreed to a call

  • 2

    closed deals

The problem

For most companies, web scraping supports the main thing they do. It is rarely the main thing. That makes ScrapeHero’s core GTM question harder than usual. Who needs this right now, and how do we find them?

What I built

  1. Watch the funding. The system monitors YC, Sequoia, Seedtable and Lightspeed for newly funded startups.
  2. Collect the context. It scrapes each startup’s site and founder data.
  3. Score the fit. That data feeds a scoring model I designed. Every startup gets a score from 0 to 100, a likely use case, and a recommended ScrapeHero service.
  4. Make it a tool. I worked with engineering to productize it as an internal tool, with PoC tracking and contact prediction.
  5. Pick who to contact. Top scores only, and the newest companies first.
  6. Write the outreach. I wrote the cold outreach copy for the filtered list.

How the score works

The model only counts what it can prove. Every startup starts at zero. Points go on when something public backs them up, like a product page, documentation, an integration or a case study. Each point is logged with the source it came from.

Absence of evidence is not evidence.

Adds 10 points
Any of ten signals that the product runs on outside data. It aggregates third-party information, say. Or it is a monitoring, intelligence or prospecting platform, an AI product that needs fresh data, or a marketplace that depends on external supply.
Takes 10 off
Internal workflow software, a product that looks self-contained, no visible use of external data, or no obvious web-data opportunity.
Confidence, 1 to 10
A second rating for how strong the evidence is. With no evidence the score is capped at 60. With fewer than two independent pieces of evidence, the startup is never recommended.
Recommended for outreach
Only at a score of 70 or more, with a confidence of 7 or more.
Comes with the score
A likely use case, such as price monitoring, market intelligence or AI training data. One recommended ScrapeHero service, or none if nothing fits. And who to contact first: the founder, then the CEO, then the head of data.

Choosing the first 100

About 2,300 AI startups went through the model. We reached out to 100 of them.

All 100 scored 90. So did others, which meant the score alone couldn’t decide. The tiebreak was age. The most recently founded companies went first.

A young company’s infrastructure is less mature, and its data sources aren’t set in stone yet. There is less to undo, so it is more open to change. I’ve written about that kind of inertia before.

Did it work?

10 of the 100 founders agreed to a call. 2 of those calls closed.

What this one shows

Most of this project isn’t content. It’s working out who is worth talking to, writing that logic down as a model, and getting it built with engineering. The copy came last.

Website & conversion

ScrapeHero website revamp

The website had not been updated in years, and it was hiding the things clients liked most about the company. I rebuilt its structure, messaging and voice.

Company
ScrapeHero
My role
Content strategy, information architecture, UX messaging
Timeline
Oct 2023 to Apr 2024
Worked with
Founders, product, sales, SEO, design

Results

  • ~20%

    lower bounce rate on high-intent pages

  • ~35%

    fewer dead clicks on the same pages

  • ~25–30%

    more traffic to money pages that had been starved of visibility

The project began in October 2023, while my title was still Content Writer. I became Content Lead that November and led it from there.

So what was wrong?

Competitors refreshed their websites regularly. ScrapeHero’s still had its mid-2010s layout, which hands a buyer an awkward question. If the website feels outdated, is the service outdated too?

  • Money pages were long and hard to get through. Educational content crowded out what the service does.
  • Different pages had different navigation bars. Labels were vague and CTAs were mislabelled, so important pages were hard to find.
  • Whole sections produced dead clicks.
  • The tone changed from page to page.

What I found

I interviewed the founders, product managers, sales and SEO. I went through Microsoft Clarity recordings and heatmaps, Search Console data, competitor sites, and the existing content page by page.

The analytics said users were overwhelmed. Rage clicks, dead clicks, high bounce rates. People couldn’t tell what the service was or what to do next.

The interviews said something more useful. Clients valued three things: the data quality, quick and proactive support, and the way ScrapeHero detects website changes and adapts on its own.

None of it was on the website.

What I changed

  • Redesigned the information architecture around capabilities, process, outcomes and proof. Educational tangents moved to the blog.
  • One navigation across the whole site, with clearer labels and interlinking that surfaced the high-value pages.
  • Rewrote the money pages. Shorter, with the process and the proof brought forward.
  • Kept SEO in step the whole way: H-tags, internal linking, keywords.
  • Worked with the designers on page flows, and acted as the go-between for product, design, SEO and leadership.

The voice that came out of it

The interviews showed that every team described the company a little differently. Some content sounded too technical, some sounded like generic marketing. So I wrote a tone of voice framework: five pillars, each with a description, do’s and don’ts, and how often to use it (always, often, occasionally).

Solution-oriented
Cut the fluff, focus on outcomes, point the reader to the fix.
Straightforward
Respect the reader’s time and intelligence.
Authoritative
Confidence that comes from expertise, facts and experience.
Reliable
Precise and honest. Commit only to what can be delivered.
Approachable
Warm and responsive, still professional.

It took the guessing out of feedback. Nobody had to ask “does this sound right?” because the answer was written down. The same guidelines are used across the website, blog and social.

What I’d do differently

  • Run the stakeholder interviews with tighter prompts. Many conversations drifted into opinion and I got to the useful parts slower than I should have.
  • Decide SEO priorities earlier. Which pages have to rank, and which can rely on people clicking through.
  • Prototype content sooner, so design and copy fit without the late back-and-forth.

One constraint I’d keep. Limited card space forced me to find the one detail that mattered and write it properly.

The next round is already underway. In 2026 I’m driving the strategy for another revamp, this time with conversion as the brief.

Content systems & SEO

ScrapeHero’s content system

The blog went from one article every few months to 15 to 20 content deliverables a week, across four content types. The same system is still in use, including at sister brands.

Company
ScrapeHero
My role
Content Lead
Timeline
Nov 2023 to present
Team
5 writers, 3 designers as secondary reports. With SEO, data analysts and developers.

Results

  • 15–20

    content deliverables a week, up from one article every few months

  • ~7.9M

    average monthly traffic after a Google update cut it from ~10M to ~3M. About 70% of the loss recovered.

  • Still running

    through several hiring cycles, and adopted by sister brands as their content operating system

So what was wrong?

Before I took over, publishing was irregular. Sometimes one article every few months. Developers wrote tutorials in their spare time. Writers were hired, got confused, and were dropped because there was no structure for them to work in. SEO happened after the fact, if at all.

Older tutorials that used to pull traffic were slipping because nobody updated them. And nothing connected an article to a business goal or a product.

More articles wouldn’t have fixed that. The company needed a system.

Four content types, each with a job

  • Top of funnel

    Technical articles

    Niche, long-tail keywords. Steady traffic, and proof that we know what we’re doing.

  • High intent

    Cloud content

    Tutorials for queries like “scrape X website”. Sign-ups for ScrapeHero Cloud, and leads for custom services.

  • Bottom of funnel

    Business articles

    For decision-makers who will never read a tutorial. These support service sales.

  • Widest reach

    Research reports

    Data reports built with the analysts, such as mapping a brand’s retail footprint across the US from POI data. Some of the most heavily backlinked pages on the site.

The ratio between them matters. Lean too hard on one category and the rest of the picture goes missing.

How it ran

  1. Quarterly plan
  2. Weekly assignment
  3. Audience assessment
  4. Draft
  5. Peer review
  6. Editor review
  7. SEO review
  8. Design
  9. Publish

Everything lived on a Basecamp board. SEO came in at planning, with keyword research, topic clustering and intent mapping, and stopped being a fix applied after the draft. Every category had its own template and brief. Declining evergreen articles were audited and rewritten as part of the routine.

Writers were trained on scraping concepts, on audience intent, and on why the business cared about each piece. Then they were left to get on with it.

Then a Google update hit

A major Google update took monthly traffic from around 10M to around 3M. Over the next few months we regained 4.5 to 5M of it. Traffic has averaged about 7.9M a month since.

Writing for AI search

As AI Overviews arrived, I adjusted the plan to go after questions they left unanswered. I also started building formats meant to be cited by AI search: comparison pages, definitional guides and FAQ content. ScrapeHero vs Bright Data is one of them. That work carries on in my current role.

A scoring system that cut repeat errors

As the team grew, I noticed writers repeating the same kinds of mistakes, including people who had been there over a year. Edits were happening. What was missing was a way for a writer to see their own patterns.

So every edit got a label. Seven error types, each weighted by business risk and not only by writing quality. The scores were summed per draft. Under 5 was a strong article. Zero was perfect.

CodeWhat it flagsWhat it points to
CEContextual errors. Content that doesn’t fit the section or topic.Gaps in research and outlining
ICOIneffective communication of offerings. Missing or badly framed value propositions.Weak understanding of what the business sells
ASIAudience suitability issues. The content doesn’t match who it’s for.Poor audience awareness

Within three months most writers were consistently scoring under 2, which made the system mostly redundant. I phased it out, as planned. It comes back whenever a new writer joins and goes away again once they’ve settled.

Good systems don’t control people, they make improvement inevitable.

What I took from it

  • When the system is predictable, the quality becomes predictable too.
  • Teaching writers how to think about audience, intent and structure is worth more than telling them what to write.
  • Writers do better with structure than with micromanagement.

Earning an audience before selling to it

Web scraping is a niche, technical service with a small natural audience on social. I led with data people wanted to share and brought the product in later. Annual YouTube views went from 2K to 150K.

Company
ScrapeHero
My role
Content Lead. I own social content end to end.
Timeline
Nov 2023 to present
Worked with
Design, data analysts

Results

  • 2K → 150K

    annual YouTube views

  • 400K+

    impressions per post on research-driven distribution with the data team

  • Zero paid

    consistent inbound leads from LinkedIn, grown organically

The problem

Promotional content underperforms on social. Heavily technical content loses anyone who isn’t a developer. And service content does badly on YouTube Shorts in particular.

So the plan was to earn attention first, and introduce scraping once there was an audience to introduce it to.

Five layers

  1. Data and statistics first. Posts built on stats from datasets that can be scraped and analysed. Stats get shared, and they show what web data can do without a sales pitch.
  2. Education. Scraping techniques and best practices for the technical crowd, plus a Web Scraping Glossary series that explains the jargon to beginners and non-technical decision-makers.
  3. Small brands analysis. A series analysing the e-commerce listings of small and mid-sized brands. It made scraping feel concrete to founders, marketers and operators.
  4. Stat-based shorts on YouTube. Short videos on trending data, picked for general interest and not for business relevance. Subscribers first, so that scraping content would later reach someone.
  5. Tutorials and business shorts. “Scrape X website” walkthroughs, and shorts on in-house scraping versus outsourcing: costs, maintenance, trade-offs. These kept the channel tied to what ScrapeHero sells.

How it runs

I write the social briefs and the video scripting templates, set the deliverables, review the visuals, and give the final go-ahead before anything is published.

What it shows

Knowing when not to sell.

Data content got the attention. Educational content built the trust. Tutorials and business content did the converting, to an audience that was already warm.

About

I studied computer science. Then I went into marketing.

What I do

I’m a content and marketing strategist. For the past three years that has meant ScrapeHero, a web data extraction company whose buyers range from developers to people who have never written a line of code and just need the data.

The work covers lead generation, the website, messaging, SEO, social, and the team that produces it all.

How I got here

I have a B.Tech in Computer Science. While I was in college I did the marketing for a few campus events, and it felt like the thing I was actually great at. I have always loved writing, and I have always appreciated great marketing. So I started out in content.

My first job was at growth.cx, where I spent eight months writing for IT consulting firms, B2B SaaS companies, D2C brands and ad-analytics platforms. Good practice for changing tone on demand.

I joined ScrapeHero in June 2023 as a content writer, on tutorials and landing pages for a developer-facing scraping product. Five months later I was leading content. The blog, the website, the brand voice, social, and a team of five writers with three designers as secondary reports.

In 2026 the job widened again, into GTM. Lead generation, messaging for new products, and getting the company cited in AI search.

How I work

  • I start with who is worth talking to. The lead generation system came out of that question, and the copy came last.
  • I don’t treat a blog, a website or a social channel as separate jobs. Each one is a system, and if it’s underperforming, something in the system is broken.
  • SEO goes in at the planning stage. It is not something to sprinkle on a finished draft.
  • I’d sooner teach a writer how to think about audience, intent and structure than tell them what to write.
  • B2B still sells to people. A person is at the end of the chain, and that person is distracted, impatient and resistant to change. I wrote a whole piece about it.

Good systems don’t control people, they make improvement inevitable.

Beyond work

I got into photography as a teenager and never left. Still very much an amateur.

I love pop culture, and the business of music most of all. The world building. The amount of effort it takes to make an album cycle look effortless, and how accidental it can be when one of them becomes a success. If I had to pick the role that intrigues me most, it’s the creative directors behind pop stars.

I’m working on my own music skills too, which is why I’ve started picking up the guitar.
